Launch Weather Dashboard

Lists the launches scheduled over the next few days and, for each, looks up the forecast at the pad for the launch hour (wind near the ground and high up, thunderstorms, rain) and the current space weather (the Sun's activity and its effect on the space near Earth). It flags anything over a few limits, which you can change. This is a screening aid, not a launch decision. Only the launch's own range weather officers, working to the vehicle's launch commit criteria, can say whether the weather allows a launch.

How this is worked out

What it is not. US ranges decide launch weather against the vehicle's launch commit criteria, including the Lightning Launch Commit Criteria, using lightning detection, electric field mills, weather radar, balloon wind profiles and cloud observations, none of which a public forecast model supplies. This page only compares model forecasts and public space-weather measurements with a few limits. Its results are indicative and must not be read as a go or no-go.

Weather at the pad

For each launch the page takes Open-Meteo's hourly forecast at the pad and uses the hour nearest the launch's earliest time (its "no earlier than" time, which often slips). Checks:

  • Surface wind, the model's sustained wind 10 m above the ground, flagged over 30 knots. The 30 kt figure is published for one vehicle: NASA's Falcon 9 / Crew Dragon launch weather criteria limit the sustained wind at the 162 ft (49 m) level of the pad. Other vehicles have other limits, and the wind at 49 m is usually stronger than at 10 m, so this is indicative.
  • Thunderstorm in the forecast (WMO weather codes 95, 96 and 99), flagged as over. It stands in for the Lightning Launch Commit Criteria, which forbid launching within 10 nautical miles of lightning for 30 minutes after it, through or near thunderstorm anvil clouds (10 nautical miles) or debris clouds (3 nautical miles), and when the surface electric field within 5 nautical miles has reached 1,500 V/m in the last 15 minutes, among other rules.
  • Upper wind at 250 hPa (about 34,000 ft, 10.4 km, in the standard atmosphere), flagged from 100 kt. Made up for this tool: real upper-level limits concern wind shear and the loads on the vehicle.
  • Chance of precipitation, flagged from 60 %. Made up for this tool: rain is not itself a launch rule, but thick cloud layers into freezing temperatures (4,500 ft or more) and "disturbed weather" are, and they often come with rain.
  • Wave height at a recovery point you give, from Open-Meteo Marine, flagged from 3 m. Made up for this tool: recovery ship and droneship sea-state limits are not published.

Space weather

From NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center (services.swpc.noaa.gov), on the NOAA Space Weather Scales:

  • Geomagnetic storms, G1 to G5, from the planetary Kp index (0 to 9, in thirds: 5- is 4.67): G1 is Kp 5, G2 Kp 6, G3 Kp 7, G4 Kp 8 (including 9-), G5 Kp 9. Below storm level SWPC describes Kp 0 to 2 as quiet, 3 as unsettled and 4 as active. The Kp shown is SWPC's latest estimated 3-hour value.
  • Solar radiation storms, S1 to S5, from the flux of protons above 10 MeV measured by GOES: 10, 100, 1,000, 10,000 and 100,000 pfu (particles per cm² per second per steradian).
  • Radio blackouts, R1 to R5, from the GOES X-ray flux at 0.1 to 0.8 nm: M1 (10⁻⁵ W/m²), M5, X1 (10⁻⁴ W/m²), X10 and X20. Flare classes A, B, C, M and X are decades of that flux from 10⁻⁸ W/m².

The forecast table is NOAA's own: a predicted G level and the chances of S1 or more, of R1 to R2 and of R3 or more, for each of the next three UTC days. A launch on one of those days is checked against the larger of the current and the predicted G level; later launches only against current conditions. The space-weather limits are made up for this tool: G4 for most launches (NOAA lists surface charging, orientation problems and more drag on satellites among G3 to G5 effects); G2 for a constellation batch released low, because in February 2022 a G1 to G2 storm raised drag enough to bring down most of a batch of Starlink satellites released at about 210 km; S3 for most launches (NOAA lists single-event upsets and noise in imaging systems at S3); S1 for crewed launches.

Published criteria, for reference

NASA's Falcon 9 / Crew Dragon launch weather criteria (fact sheet, 2020) include: sustained wind at the 162 ft level of the pad over 30 kt; upper-level wind shear that could cause control problems; flight through a cloud layer 4,500 ft thick or more that extends into freezing temperatures; the lightning, anvil, debris cloud, disturbed weather and field mill rules above; and weather limits at the abort and recovery areas. See the fact sheet for the full rules and their exceptions. Most other launch providers and agencies do not publish their weather criteria.

Data

Launch Library 2 by The Space Devs (ll.thespacedevs.com, API 2.2.0; its free tier allows about 15 requests an hour, and each button press uses one). Its "weather probability" is the launch's published chance of favourable weather where one exists. Open-Meteo forecast and marine APIs (api.open-meteo.com, marine-api.open-meteo.com). NOAA SWPC JSON products: planetary K index, NOAA scales, alerts, GOES integral protons and X-rays. All are fetched straight from the browser; if a service refuses, the page says which. The kind of mission is guessed from the launch's name and type (for example "Starlink" is a constellation batch); change it in the launch's details.
