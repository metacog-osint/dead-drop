Launch Window Calculator

An orbit's plane stays nearly fixed in space while the Earth turns beneath it, so a launch site passes under the plane at set times. A rocket launched at one of those moments can fly straight into the plane without spending fuel to turn. This tool lists those moments for a site and a target orbit: normally twice a day, once heading north-east and once south-east (north-west and south-west for a retrograde orbit). The plane is set either by its right ascension of the ascending node (RAAN, where it crosses the equator going north, measured in the sky from the vernal equinox) or, for a sun-synchronous orbit, by the local time of that node.

How this is worked out

The site lies in the orbit's plane when its local sidereal time (LST, the right ascension crossing its meridian: the Greenwich mean sidereal time plus the site's east longitude) equals

LST = RAAN + Δλ (north-going pass)    or    LST = RAAN + 180° − Δλ (south-going pass)
sin Δλ = tan(latitude) / tan(inclination)

Δλ is the angle along the equator from the ascending node to the site's meridian, from the right spherical triangle of the equator, the orbit and the meridian. Greenwich mean sidereal time comes from Meeus, equation 12.4: 280.46061837° + 360.98564736629° × (days since 2000 January 1, 12h UT), plus two tiny century terms. It gains 360.9856° a day, so the sky returns to the same place every sidereal day, 23 h 56 min 4.09 s. Each pass therefore comes about 4 minutes earlier each day, and the two passes of a day are (180° − 2Δλ) / 360.9856° of a day apart. When the inclination equals the latitude, the two passes merge into one a day; below it, the site never sees the plane. The launch azimuth shown is the inertial one, sin Az = cos(inclination) / cos(latitude); the Launch Azimuth Calculator gives the heading corrected for the Earth's rotation.

The plane is not quite fixed: the Earth's equatorial bulge (the J2 term) turns it by −1.5 n J2 (R/a)² cos i radians per second (n is the orbit's mean motion, R = 6,378.137 km, J2 = 1.08263 × 10⁻³), about −5° a day for the ISS, which brings its windows about 23 minutes earlier each day instead of 4. A sun-synchronous orbit is built so its plane turns with the mean sun, 0.9856° a day; its node is set by its local mean solar time, RAAN = right ascension of the mean sun + 15° × (node time − 12 h), and its windows fall at the same local mean time every day.

Limits. These are the instants the site passes through the plane. A real window is offset by a few minutes, because the Earth keeps turning during the climb to orbit, and widened by however much the rocket can steer sideways; it is narrowed or set by other things this tool does not know: rendezvous phasing (a launch to meet the ISS is usually a single instant chosen so the vehicles line up), the Sun angle a payload needs, range safety and weather. UT1 is taken as UTC (within 0.9 s). A RAAN from a two-line element set is in the TEME frame, which pairs with Greenwich mean sidereal time as CelesTrak's documentation of SGP4 describes; the J2 drift here is the mean secular rate only. Node time uses the mean sun; the true sun differs by up to 16 minutes (the equation of time).

References: Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms, 2nd ed., chapter 12; Vallado, Fundamentals of Astrodynamics and Applications, 4th ed., launch windows; Curtis, Orbital Mechanics for Engineering Students, section 4.7 (oblateness). Test: tests/launch-window.test.js (Meeus's examples 12.a and 12.b as window times; the two daily windows from latitude 28.5° to 51.6° are 30,880.6 s apart and sum to a sidereal day).
