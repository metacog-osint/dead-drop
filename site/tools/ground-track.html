Ground Track Plotter

Draws a satellite's ground track, the path traced on the Earth by the point directly beneath it, for one or more orbits from a chosen time, and lists the points with their height above the Earth. Each orbit the track shifts west, because the Earth turns underneath it.

An optional name line, then line 1 and line 2. Get a current one with the TLE Analyzer.

How this is worked out

The SGP4 model (satellite.js 5.0.0, MIT licence) moves the element set to each time, giving the satellite's position in the TEME frame (an inertial frame tied to the Earth's equator, which does not turn with the Earth). That position is turned into Earth-fixed coordinates by rotating it through the Greenwich mean sidereal time (GMST, how far the Earth has turned against the stars):

GMST (s) = 67310.54841 + (876600 h + 8640184.812866 s) T + 0.093104 T² − 6.2×10⁻⁶ T³

with T the Julian centuries since 1 January 2000, 12:00. The Earth-fixed position is then turned into geodetic latitude, longitude and height above the WGS 84 ellipsoid by iteration. Where two neighbouring points lie either side of the 180° meridian, the line is ended at the meridian and restarted on the other side, so the map does not draw it the long way round. "Westward shift per orbit" is the Earth's turn during one period (360.9856° a day times the period); the drift of the orbit's plane is left out of that figure but not out of the track.

Limits. SGP4 positions drift from the truth as the time moves away from the element set's epoch: typically about a kilometre at the epoch and growing by one to a few kilometres a day for low orbits (a rough guide). UTC stands in for UT1 (under 0.9 s difference, under 0.5 km on the ground) and polar motion is ignored. The lines between points are straight on the flat map; a short step keeps them close to the true curve. Map tiles come from OpenStreetMap (tile.openstreetmap.org); no other network service is used.

References: Vallado, Fundamentals of Astrodynamics and Applications, 4th ed., section 3.5 (sidereal time) and Algorithm 12 (geodetic coordinates); Vallado et al., "Revisiting Spacetrack Report #3", AIAA 2006-6753 (TEME). Test: tests/ground-track.test.js (Vallado Example 3-5: GMST 152.578787810° at 12:14 UT1 on 20 August 1992; Example 3-3: 34.352496° N, 46.4464° E, 5,085.22 km; a 90-minute orbit moves 22.5616° west each revolution; a segment from 170° E to 170° W splits at 180°).
