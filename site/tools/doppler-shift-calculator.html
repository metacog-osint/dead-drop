Doppler Shift Calculator

When a transmitter moves toward you its signal arrives slightly higher in frequency, and when it moves away, slightly lower. This works out that shift from the speed along the line between you and the transmitter, and, for a satellite in a circular orbit, the largest shift a ground station can see during a pass.

How this is worked out

The range rate is how fast the distance between transmitter and receiver changes, positive when it grows. With speed v at angle α from the line of sight, range rate = −v cos α, and the shift is

Δf = −f × (range rate) / c

so an approaching transmitter (negative range rate) is received high. During a satellite pass the received frequency therefore starts high, passes through the transmitted frequency at closest approach, and ends low: tune downward as the pass goes on. This is the first-order formula; the relativistic correction is less than one part in a billion at orbital speeds.

For a circular orbit of radius r = R + altitude, the orbital speed is v = √(μ/r). On a pass straight overhead, the share of that speed along your line of sight is v sin η, where η, the angle at the satellite between you and straight down, satisfies sin η = (R / r) cos(elevation). It is largest at the horizon, v R / r, and zero overhead. The Earth's rotation carries you at up to 0.465 km/s (at the equator; times the cosine of your latitude elsewhere), so no pass can show a line-of-sight speed above v R / r + ωE R cos(latitude). The tool gives that bound; the true peak for a given pass lies below it.

Limits. Spherical, non-rotating Earth for the overhead-pass figures, with the rotation allowance added only to the bound. For an exact shift curve over a real pass, compute the range rate from the satellite's orbital elements. For high orbits the overhead-pass figures mean little: a geostationary satellite's shift comes from its small daily drift, not from v R / r. Constants: c = 299,792.458 km/s, μ = 398,600.4418 km³/s² and ωE = 7.292115 × 10⁻⁵ rad/s (WGS 84), R = 6,371 km.

References: Rappaport, Wireless Communications, 2nd ed., section 5.2.1; Vallado, Fundamentals of Astrodynamics and Applications, 4th ed., chapter 4 (range rate); Curtis, Orbital Mechanics for Engineering Students, section 2.6; Wertz and Larson, Space Mission Analysis and Design, 3rd ed., section 5.2. Test: tests/doppler-shift.test.js (137.5 MHz at 7.5 km/s head-on gives +3,439.88 Hz; a 550 km orbit gives at most 10,194.8 Hz at 437.5 MHz over a non-rotating Earth).
