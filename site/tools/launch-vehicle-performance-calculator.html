Launch Vehicle Performance Calculator

A first estimate of how much a rocket can carry to an orbit, scaled from what its maker says it can carry to low Earth orbit. The tool works out the extra delta-v (the change of speed the vehicle must supply) to reach the target instead of the reference orbit, then converts that into payload with a rule of thumb. The delta-v figures are textbook physics; the payload figure is an approximation and can be off by 10 per cent or more. Use a vehicle's own user's guide for anything that matters.

How this is worked out

Delta-v (physics, ideal). Every step is an impulsive velocity change with no gravity, drag or steering losses, which are about the same for the reference and the target ascents and so largely cancel (a real climb to low orbit needs about 9.3 to 9.5 km/s in all, of which 1.5 to 2 km/s is losses).

  • Earth's rotation. The speed to add relative to the ground is √(v² − 2 v ω N cos i + (ω N cos φ)²): v is the orbital speed, ω N cos φ the site's eastward speed and ω N cos i the part of it that lies along the orbit (from cos i = cos φ sin Az). A due-east launch gains the most; a polar one gains none; a retrograde one loses.
  • Altitude. A Hohmann transfer between circular orbits: two burns, √(μ(2/r₁ − 1/a)) − √(μ/r₁) and √(μ/r₂) − √(μ(2/r₂ − 1/a)), with a = (r₁ + r₂)/2.
  • GTO is the transfer orbit from the reference altitude to 35,786 km, at the site's latitude; GEO adds the apogee burn that circularises and removes the inclination together, √(va² + vGEO² − 2 va vGEO cos i). Makers' GTO figures often use a different perigee or a partly removed inclination, so compare like with like.
  • Below the site's latitude the vehicle launches at the latitude and then turns the plane in the final orbit, 2 v sin(Δi/2). That is an upper bound; doing it at a higher point or during the climb is cheaper.
  • Sun-synchronous inclination is where the Earth's bulge (J2) turns the orbit 0.9856° a day, keeping pace with the Sun: cos i = −(0.9856°/day) / (1.5 n J2 (R/a)²).

Payload (rule of thumb). The extra delta-v is paid for by turning payload into upper-stage propellant: the stage's burnout mass shrinks by the rocket equation, P = (P₀ + mdry) e−Δv / (g₀ Isp) − mdry, with g₀ = 9.80665 m/s². This is not a vehicle model: it ignores tank size, restarts, coast, the lower stages and the change in losses, and a lower target can show more payload than the vehicle's structure allows. Against the makers' GTO figures it lands within about 10 per cent for the listed vehicles (see the table). The vehicle figures are approximate public numbers, not checked against the users' guides; reference orbits differ by vehicle, and "expendable" means no booster recovery.

References: Vallado, Fundamentals of Astrodynamics and Applications, 4th ed., chapter 6 (Hohmann transfers, example 6-1; plane changes); Curtis, Orbital Mechanics for Engineering Students, chapter 6 and section 4.7; Wertz and Larson, Space Mission Analysis and Design, 3rd ed., chapter 6 (sun-synchronous orbits); WGS 84 constants.
