How this is worked out

Air gets thinner with height, so its refractive index falls and a ray bends slightly back toward the ground. The standard way to allow for this is to draw the ray straight and make the Earth bigger: an effective radius a = k R, with R the Earth's mean radius (6,371 km). For radio waves in an average atmosphere k = 4/3 (ITU-R Recommendations P.453 and P.834, the basis of the path profiles in P.452 and P.526). For light, a refraction coefficient of about 1/7 gives k = 7/6; surveyors commonly use 0.13 to 0.14. k = 1 is the bare geometry.

horizon d = √(2 k R h)    d (km) = 4.12 √(h in m) for k = 4/3, 3.86 √h for k = 7/6, 3.57 √h for k = 1

Two heights can see each other out to the sum of their horizons. At a shorter distance D the page works out how high the straight line between the two tops passes above the smooth surface at every point between them; for distance x from A that is hA + (hB − hA) x / D − x (D − x) / (2 a). The last term is the Earth bulge: at a point d1 from one end and d2 from the other it is d1 d2 / (2 a), which in metres for d in km is d1 d2 / (12.74 k), about d1 d2 / 17 for radio.

first Fresnel zone radius r1 = √(λ d1 d2 / d) = 17.31 √(d1 d2 / (f d))   (r in m, d in km, f in GHz)

The first Fresnel zone is the region around the straight line in which a reflected or diffracted path is less than half a wavelength longer than the direct one (ITU-R P.530 and P.526; the coefficient is often rounded to 17.3 or 17.32, which takes the speed of light as 300,000 km/s; this page uses the exact value). Keeping 60 % of r1 clear of the ground leaves the extra loss from diffraction close to 0 dB, which is why link planners use it. The page checks 0.6 r1 against the smooth surface along the whole path and, when it is not met, gives the height at B that would meet it.

Limits:

  • The Earth is smooth here: no hills, buildings or trees. Over land, use a terrain profile (the Viewshed Calculator does this for sight lines) and add the bulge to the ground heights.
  • k varies with the weather. Radio links are often planned for k as low as 2/3 for part of the time, and temperature inversions over water can trap signals well past the horizon (ducting). Near the ground, light bends by very different amounts on a cold, still morning and a hot afternoon.
  • Heights are above the local surface, not above sea level, unless the path is over sea. The horizon distance is the straight-line tangent distance; it equals the distance along the ground to well under 0.01 % at these heights.
  • Optical visibility also depends on haze and on how small the target looks; this page gives only the geometry.

The page uses no outside service and no library: the maths is in lib/radio-horizon.js.

References: ITU-R P.453 and P.834 (refractivity, effective Earth radius factor k = 4/3); ITU-R P.452 and P.526 (effective-Earth path profiles, diffraction, 0.6 r1 clearance); ITU-R P.530 (Fresnel zone radius for line-of-sight links). Test: tests/radio-horizon.test.js (a 10 m antenna's radio horizon is 13.03 km; 30 m and 10 m see each other to 35.61 km; r1 at the middle of a 10 km, 2.4 GHz path is 17.67 m; the bulge at the middle of a 50 km path is 36.79 m).
