RF Source Triangulation

Estimates where a radio transmitter is from measurements made at several receivers: either the direction each receiver heard it from (its bearing, or angle of arrival), or the differences in the times the signal reached each receiver (time difference of arrival, TDOA). It gives a best position and an error ellipse, the area that should hold the transmitter 95 times in 100 if your stated measurement errors are right.

The typical error of your direction finder, one standard deviation.
0 if bearings are from true north. Added to every bearing.
One nanosecond is 30 cm of range. Arrival times are entered in microseconds (µs).

    How this is worked out

    Positions are on a sphere of radius 6,371 km, with latitudes and longitudes in decimal degrees (WGS 84). Distances, bearings and where two bearing lines cross use the great-circle formulas collected by Chris Veness. A bearing line is a great circle, the shortest path on the globe, not a straight line on the map.

    Bearings. Each pair of bearings is crossed to give a starting point. The fix is then the position that best fits every bearing at once (weighted least squares, after Stansfield): each bearing counts in proportion to how precisely it places the line at that range, since a bearing error of σ moves the line sideways by about range × σ. With two bearings the fix is where they cross; the ellipse comes from the stated error, not from the spread of crossings. The cut angle is the angle at which two lines cross: near 90° is best, under 30° stretches the fix along the lines.

    Time differences. The first receiver is the reference. Each other receiver's arrival time minus the reference's, times the speed of light, is how much farther that receiver is from the transmitter. The tool searches a grid around the receivers, refines every promising point by least squares, and keeps the best. Three receivers can leave two positions that fit exactly; the tool reports both, and a fourth receiver settles it.

    Checking the fit. χ² sums each measurement's misfit squared over its stated error squared. With more measurements than the two unknowns, χ² should be near the degrees of freedom (measurements minus 2). Much larger means the measurements disagree by more than your stated errors allow: a wrong bearing, a reflection, or errors larger than you think.

    What accuracy to expect. The ellipse is only as honest as the errors you enter, and it assumes they are random and unbiased. Each degree of bearing error moves a line sideways by about 1.75% of the range: 175 m at 10 km, 1.75 km at 100 km. Real direction-finding errors are often several degrees, and in towns, hills or near reflecting structures they can be 10° or more and biased rather than random; use the figure measured for your own equipment where you have one (these typical figures are indicative, not a specification). For TDOA, each nanosecond of timing error is 30 cm of range; receivers timed from GNSS commonly agree to tens of nanoseconds, and how precisely an arrival time can be measured also depends on the signal's bandwidth (wider is sharper) and on reflections. Either way the error grows quickly when the transmitter is outside the area the receivers surround.

    Limits. Two dimensions: the transmitter is assumed to be at ground level, so an airborne one biases the fix. The sphere differs from the true ellipsoid by up to about 0.3% in distance. A fix from noisy bearings can sit off the true position by more than the ellipse when errors are biased. Treat the result as a search area, not a location.
