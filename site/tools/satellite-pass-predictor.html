Satellite Pass Predictor

Lists a satellite's passes over a place: each time it rises above the horizon, climbs to its highest elevation (angle above the horizon), and sets again, with the compass directions. It also marks which passes could be seen with the eye: the satellite in sunlight while your sky is dark.

How this is worked out

The SGP4 model (satellite.js 5.0.0, MIT licence) moves the element set through the period, and each moment's position is turned into azimuth and elevation from your place on the WGS 84 ellipsoid (as in the Satellite Visibility Calculator). The search steps every 30 seconds. Where the elevation crosses the horizon mask between two steps, it halves the interval until the crossing is known to half a second; the highest point is then refined by a golden-section search around the highest step. A pass that pokes above the mask for less than one step can be missed, but such a pass only grazes the mask.

"Seen by eye" checks every 30 seconds of the pass: the satellite out of the Earth's umbra (full shadow, by a conical shadow model) and the Sun 6° or more below your horizon. That is a common rule, not a brightness model: small satellites can meet it and still be too faint to see. Refraction near the horizon is not applied.

SGP4's error grows with time from the element set's epoch, typically one to a few kilometres a day for low orbits (a rough guide). A low satellite covers about 7.5 km a second, so each 7.5 km of error along its track moves rise and set by about a second, and passes a week or two ahead can shift by tens of seconds or more. Use a fresh element set.

References: Vallado, Fundamentals of Astrodynamics and Applications, 4th ed., sections 4.4 (look angles) and 11.4 (rise and set by root bracketing); Montenbruck and Pfleger, Astronomy on the Personal Computer, section 3.3. Test: tests/satellite-passes.test.js (a made-up sine-shaped elevation that rises at 500 s and sets at 2500 s, and a circular equatorial orbit 500 km up whose overhead pass lasts 742.2 s).
