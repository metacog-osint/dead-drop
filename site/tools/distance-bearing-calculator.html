How this is worked out

The Earth is treated as a sphere of radius 6,371 km (the mean radius). The distance uses the haversine formula, which stays accurate for short distances:

a = sin²(Δφ/2) + cos φA cos φB sin²(Δλ/2)    d = 2R · atan2(√a, √(1−a))

where φ is latitude and λ longitude. The initial bearing is

θ = atan2(sin Δλ cos φB, cos φA sin φB − sin φA cos φB cos Δλ)

and the final bearing is the bearing from B back to A turned through 180°. The midpoint is the point halfway along the great circle, not the average of the coordinates. Nautical miles use 1 NM = 1.852 km and statute miles 1 mi = 1.609344 km, both exact.

Limits. A sphere is not the Earth's true shape (an ellipsoid, WGS 84); distances on the sphere differ from ellipsoidal ones by up to about 0.5%, and bearings by a fraction of a degree. Use an ellipsoidal method (Vincenty or Karney) for survey or navigation work. Bearings are from true north, not magnetic north. When the two points are on exactly opposite sides of the Earth, every direction is a shortest path, so no bearing or midpoint is given. The known places are approximate launch-site positions, good to a few kilometres.

Reference: Chris Veness, "Calculate distance, bearing and more between latitude/longitude points", movable-type.co.uk. Test: tests/distance-bearing.test.js (Land's End to John o' Groats: 968.9 km, initial bearing 009° 07′ 11″, final bearing 011° 16′ 31″).
