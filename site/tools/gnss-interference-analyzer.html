GNSS Interference Indicators

Satellite-navigation (GNSS) signals arrive very weak, so other radio signals can drown them (jamming) or imitate them (spoofing). This checklist reads the figures a receiver reports, ideally against that same receiver's normal readings at the same place, and lists which indicators are flagged and what else could cause them. It is a checklist, not a detector: it cannot confirm jamming or spoofing.

How this is worked out

C/N0, the carrier-to-noise density ratio, is the signal power over the noise power in one hertz of bandwidth. Interference adds to the noise, so the receiver sees C / (N0 + I0) instead of C / N0. If the satellites' signal power is unchanged, a drop of D dB from the receiver's normal C/N0 therefore means the noise plus interference rose by D dB, and the interference on its own is 10D/10 − 1 times the thermal noise. The tool shows that figure in dB: a 3 dB drop means interference about equal to the noise; a 10 dB drop means about nine times the noise (9.5 dB).

Satellites. A three-dimensional fix needs at least four satellites, because the receiver solves for four unknowns: position in three axes and its own clock error. That threshold is physics. Every other threshold here is indicative: a round number near commonly quoted open-sky values, chosen for this checklist, not taken from a standard:

  • C/N0 with no normal entered: 40 dB-Hz or more reads as normal, 35 to 40 as low, under 35 as well below (open-sky GPS L1 C/A is commonly 40 to 50 dB-Hz; satellites low in the sky and obstructions read lower).
  • C/N0 against normal: down 3 dB or more is flagged, 10 dB or more strongly; up 3 dB or more is flagged as a possible sign of false signals, which are often stronger than real ones.
  • Satellites with no normal entered: under 6 with one constellation, or under 10 with several; against normal, a quarter or more lost is flagged and half or more strongly.
  • Horizontal accuracy: worse than 10 m is flagged and worse than 20 m strongly; against normal, twice and five times the usual figure. Receivers compute this figure from their own error model, and a good spoofer can leave it looking normal.

The ticked observations are signs listed in the spoofing literature and in receiver guidance. A fall in the automatic gain control means more power is arriving in the band, a strong sign of interference when the receiver reports it. The pattern line says whether the flags look more like lost signals (interference) or false signals (spoofing). There is no overall score: the old weighted score could not be defended.

Limits. Every indicator has innocent causes: buildings, foliage, antenna or cable faults, unwanted emissions from nearby electronics, receiver resets, and ionospheric scintillation (rapid fading caused by the upper atmosphere, common near the equator and at high latitudes). Readings from one receiver at one time cannot show where interference comes from or whether it was deliberate. Corroborate with other receivers, other times and published interference monitoring before reporting anything.

References: Kaplan and Hegarty, Understanding GPS: Principles and Applications, 2nd ed., chapter 6 (interference and effective C/N0) and chapter 2 (four satellites for a fix); Humphreys and others, "Assessing the spoofing threat", ION GNSS 2008 (spoofing signs). Test: tests/gnss-interference.test.js (a 10 dB drop in C/N0 implies interference 9.54 dB above the noise).
