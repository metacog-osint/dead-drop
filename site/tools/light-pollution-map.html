Light Pollution Map

Helps find dark places to watch satellites or the night sky. The map can show NASA's satellite image of the Earth's lights at night, which marks the towns and industry that light up the sky nearby. You can keep a list of candidate sites, and convert a sky-brightness reading (from a Sky Quality Meter, in magnitudes per square arcsecond) or the faintest star you can see into the usual measures.

How this is worked out

The map layer. The night-lights layer is NASA's VIIRS City Lights 2012 image: a cloud-free composite of light seen from orbit by the Day/Night Band of the VIIRS instrument on the Suomi NPP satellite in April and October 2012, made by NASA's Earth Observatory with NOAA's National Geophysical Data Center and served by NASA's Global Imagery Browse Services (GIBS). It shows light going up from the ground, not how bright the sky looks from a place: the glow of a city spreads across the sky tens to a few hundred kilometres away, so a site that is dark on this image can still have a bright horizon. It is also from 2012, before most towns changed to LED lighting, which the VIIRS band sees poorly in the blue. Treat it as a guide to where the lights are, then check the sky on site. NASA's imagery has no restriction on reuse; NASA asks that it be credited, as the map does. The image goes to zoom 8, so at closer zoom it is enlarged.

Sky brightness. A reading m in magnitudes per square arcsecond converts to luminance by

L (cd/m²) = 10.8 × 10⁴ × 10−0.4 m

and its ratio to a natural sky of 22.0 mag/arcsec² is 10−0.4 (m − 22.0). A natural sky works out to 171 µcd/m² by this conversion (Falchi and colleagues quote 174 µcd/m²). The natural sky itself varies by a few tenths of a magnitude with airglow, the Milky Way and the solar cycle, so a reading a little above 22.0 is not an error.

Bortle class. John Bortle's nine classes describe what an observer sees, and he gave each a range of naked-eye limiting magnitude; this tool looks your figure up in his ranges, putting a value that falls between two ranges in the brighter class. Limiting magnitude depends on the observer's eyes, experience and patience, so the class is a rough guide. There is no agreed conversion from a meter reading to a Bortle class (published tables differ by half a class or more), so the tool does not offer one.

Limits and services. The map uses OpenStreetMap tiles (tile.openstreetmap.org) and NASA GIBS tiles (gibs.earthdata.nasa.gov). Nothing else is fetched; your sites and inputs are kept only in this browser.
