How this is worked out

The SGP4 model (satellite.js 5.0.0, MIT licence) moves the element set to the chosen time and gives the satellite's position in the TEME frame, an Earth-centred frame that does not turn with the Earth. The position is turned into the Earth-fixed frame through Greenwich mean sidereal time (IAU-82). Your place is set on the WGS 84 ellipsoid, and the line from you to the satellite is split into east, north and up parts: azimuth = atan2(east, north), elevation = asin(up / range).

The Sun's direction comes from a low-precision solar formula good to about 0.01°. Whether sunlight reaches the satellite uses a conical shadow: the Sun and the Earth are seen as discs from the satellite. When the Earth's disc covers the whole Sun the satellite is in the umbra (full shadow); when it covers part, the penumbra (partial shadow); otherwise it is sunlit. The Earth is a sphere of radius 6,378 km here and the atmosphere's dimming near the edge of the shadow is ignored.

"Can be seen with the eye" means: above the horizon, not in the umbra, and the Sun at least 6° below your horizon (the end of civil twilight). That is a common rule, not a brightness model: a small or distant satellite can meet it and still be too faint to see, and a bright one can sometimes be seen in a lighter sky. Refraction, which lifts objects near the horizon by about half a degree, is not applied.

SGP4's error grows with time from the element set's epoch, typically one to a few kilometres a day for low orbits (a rough guide), so the page warns when the chosen time is more than 7 days from the epoch.

References: Vallado, Fundamentals of Astrodynamics and Applications, 4th ed., sections 3.2 and 3.5 (WGS 84, sidereal time), 4.4 (look angles), Algorithm 29 (Sun), 5.3 (shadow); Montenbruck and Gill, Satellite Orbits, section 3.4.2 (conical shadow).
