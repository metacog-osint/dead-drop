Multi-Satellite Tracker

Shows where several satellites are right now, as the point on the ground directly beneath each one, with the ground track (that point's path) it has just flown and is about to fly. Positions are worked out in your browser from each satellite's element set and move every five seconds.

How this is worked out

The SGP4 model (satellite.js 5.0.0, MIT licence) moves each element set to the current time, giving the satellite's position in the TEME frame (an inertial frame tied to the Earth's equator). That position is turned through the Greenwich mean sidereal time (how far the Earth has turned) into Earth-fixed coordinates and then into latitude, longitude and height above the WGS 84 ellipsoid, as in the Ground Track Plotter. Trails are sampled once a minute and split where they cross the 180° meridian. Ground speed is how fast the point beneath the satellite moves over the surface (a sphere of 6,371 km); speed in the copied results is the satellite's own speed in the inertial frame, which is higher.

Limits. An element set ages: SGP4 positions are typically within about a kilometre at the element set's epoch and drift by one to a few kilometres a day for low orbits (a rough guide), more for objects low enough to feel the atmosphere strongly. The table gives each set's age and marks those over 7 days old. A satellite that manoeuvres after its epoch will not be where the element set says. UTC stands in for UT1.

Network. Map tiles come from OpenStreetMap (tile.openstreetmap.org). A lookup asks CelesTrak (celestrak.org, its public GP data) for the newest element set for that catalog number, only when you press a lookup button; answers are reused for two hours, which is about how often CelesTrak updates. The tracked list is kept only in this browser.

References: Vallado, Fundamentals of Astrodynamics and Applications, 4th ed., section 3.5 and Algorithm 12; Chris Veness, "Calculate distance, bearing and more between latitude/longitude points", movable-type.co.uk (haversine). Test: tests/multi-satellite.test.js (a made-up 90-minute equatorial orbit 7,000 km from the Earth's centre: ground speed 6.948 km/s, its past hour's trail split once at 180°; Veness's Land's End to John o' Groats, 968.9 km).

Dead Drop Toolbox. Everything runs in your browser; nothing you type is sent anywhere. Lookups send only the catalog number to CelesTrak.
