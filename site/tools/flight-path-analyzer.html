Flight Path Analyzer

From a launch point, a direction (the azimuth, degrees clockwise from true north) and a ground range, this draws the path over the ground (the ground track) and gives the end point. For a ballistic flight it also gives the textbook minimum-energy trajectory for that range: the one that needs the lowest speed at engine cut-off, with how high it climbs (its apogee) and how long it takes.

How this is worked out

Ground track. The Earth is a sphere of radius 6,371 km. The end point is the destination point a distance d along the great circle that leaves the launch point on the azimuth:

φ2 = asin(sin φ1 cos δ + cos φ1 sin δ cos θ)    λ2 = λ1 + atan2(sin θ sin δ cos φ1, cos δ − sin φ1 sin φ2)

with δ = d / R. The track is drawn through a point every 50 km or so. The arrival bearing is the direction of travel at the end point.

Minimum-energy trajectory. In the free-flight phase a ballistic object follows part of an ellipse with the Earth's centre at one focus. With Ψ the range angle at the Earth's centre (range / R), the lowest speed v at cut-off that reaches the range, and the climb angle above the horizontal that it needs, are

Q = v² R / μ = 2 sin(Ψ/2) / (1 + sin(Ψ/2))    climb angle = (180° − Ψ) / 4

with μ = 398,600.4418 km³/s². The ellipse has semi-major axis a = R / (2 − Q) and eccentricity e² = 1 + Q(Q − 2) cos²(climb angle); the apogee is a(1 + e) from the Earth's centre, and the flight time comes from Kepler's equation between cut-off and re-entry. For short ranges this tends to the flat-Earth result: a 45° shot that rises to a quarter of its range.

Limits. The ballistic figures are an idealisation, not a prediction for any real vehicle: cut-off and re-entry are taken at the surface, with no atmosphere, no powered ascent and a non-rotating Earth. Real missiles often fly lofted (higher, shorter) or depressed (lower, faster) paths, and the powered phase adds height and time; real apogees for a given range can be well above or below the figure here. On the real, rotating Earth the path over the ground bends away from this great circle and the speed, angle and time needed change, by amounts that grow with flight time: the surface at the equator moves at about 465 m/s, some 840 km in a 30-minute flight. A sphere, not the WGS 84 ellipsoid: positions can differ by up to about 0.5% of the range. Map tiles come from OpenStreetMap (tile.openstreetmap.org); nothing else is sent anywhere.

References: Chris Veness, "Calculate distance, bearing and more between latitude/longitude points", movable-type.co.uk (destination point); Bate, Mueller and White, Fundamentals of Astrodynamics, chapter 6 (ballistic missile trajectories).
