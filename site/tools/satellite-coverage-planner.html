Satellite Coverage Planner

Finds the coverage windows of one satellite over a ground target: the times when the satellite stands at or above a chosen minimum elevation angle (how high above the target's horizon it must be to be useful, for a sensor or a radio link). It adds up how much of the period is covered, the longest gap, and the mean revisit time (start of one window to the start of the next), and maps the circle the satellite's ground point must be inside.

An optional name line, then line 1 and line 2. Get a current one with the TLE Analyzer.

North and east positive.

0 is the horizon; 10 to 30 is usual for links and imaging.

Results

The shaded circle is where the satellite's ground point must be for the target to see it at the minimum elevation, at the satellite's height at the start. The coloured lines are the ground track during the first windows.

How this is worked out

The SGP4 model (satellite.js 5.0.0, MIT licence) moves the element set through the period, and each moment's position is turned into elevation as seen from the target on the WGS 84 ellipsoid, as in the Satellite Pass Predictor. The search steps every 30 seconds; where the elevation crosses the minimum between two steps it is narrowed to a tenth of a second, and the highest point of each window is refined. A window is one such stretch at or above the minimum elevation. Covered time is the sum of the windows; the longest gap counts the time before the first window and after the last.

The circle on the map uses a spherical Earth (radius 6,371 km). For a satellite at altitude h seen at minimum elevation ε, the angle at the satellite off straight down (η) and the angle at the Earth's centre from the target to the circle's edge (λ) are

sin η = (R / (R + h)) cos ε    λ = 90° − ε − η

and the circle's radius along the surface is R λ (λ in radians). Its points come from the destination-point formula on the sphere. A circle that holds a pole is closed along the pole.

Limits. SGP4 positions drift from the truth as time moves from the element set's epoch, typically one to a few kilometres a day for low orbits (a rough guide); a low satellite covers about 7.5 km a second, so window times a week out can shift by tens of seconds or more. The circle is drawn for the height at the start; for an orbit that is not circular its size changes around the orbit, while the windows themselves use the true height at each moment. The sphere and the ellipsoid differ slightly, so a track can graze the circle's edge a few kilometres from where a window opens. Terrain, buildings, the sensor's own field of view, daylight and weather are not modelled. Map tiles come from OpenStreetMap (tile.openstreetmap.org); no other network service is used.

References: Wertz and Larson, Space Mission Analysis and Design, 3rd ed., sections 5.2 (coverage geometry) and 7.2 (coverage figures of merit); Vallado, Fundamentals of Astrodynamics and Applications, 4th ed., section 4.4 (look angles); Chris Veness, "Calculate distance, bearing and more between latitude/longitude points", movable-type.co.uk (destination point). Test: tests/satellite-coverage.test.js (500 km at 10°: circle radius 1,563.0 km; geostationary at 10°: 71.44° of arc; a 500 km equatorial orbit over an equatorial target: windows of 473.80 s every 6,067.27 s).
