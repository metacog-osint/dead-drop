Eclipse Prediction Calculator

How many minutes of each orbit a satellite spends in the Earth's shadow, with no sunlight on its solar panels. It depends on the orbit's altitude and its beta angle: how far the Sun stands out of the plane of the orbit. Enter the beta angle, or work it out from the orbit's tilt, its orientation and a date.

How this is worked out

The Earth's shadow is taken as a cylinder of radius R = 6,378.137 km stretching away from the Sun. For a circular orbit of radius r = R + h, the share of each orbit spent inside it is

f = (1/180°) · arccos( √(h² + 2Rh) / (r · cos β) )

and there is no eclipse at all when |β| is at or above the critical beta angle β* = arcsin(R / r). The eclipse lasts f times the orbital period, T = 2π√(r³/μ), with μ = 398,600.4418 km³/s².

When the beta angle is worked out, it is the angle between the Sun's direction and the orbit plane: β = arcsin(cos L sin i sin Ω − sin L cos ε sin i cos Ω + sin L sin ε cos i), with i the inclination, Ω the RAAN, L the Sun's ecliptic longitude and ε the tilt of the Earth's axis (obliquity), both from the Astronomical Almanac's low-precision formulae (about 0.01° from 1950 to 2050). A positive beta means the Sun is on the north side of the orbit.

Limits: circular orbits only; the shadow is a cylinder, so the partly shadowed edge (the penumbra, which adds several seconds at each end in low orbit) and the bending of sunlight by the atmosphere are left out; the Sun is taken as fixed during one orbit. The beta angle changes from day to day as the Sun moves and the orbit's node drifts, so a result holds for the date entered, not the whole year.

References: Gilmore (ed.), Spacecraft Thermal Control Handbook, Vol. I, 2nd ed., chapter 2 (eclipse fraction and beta angle); Vallado, Fundamentals of Astrodynamics and Applications, 4th ed., chapter 5 (eclipses, cylindrical shadow); Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms, 2nd ed., chapter 25 (the Sun's position). Test: tests/eclipse-prediction.test.js (550 km: period 95.650 min; at β = 30° the eclipse lasts 33.583 min; no eclipse above β = 67.016°; Meeus's example 25.a for the Sun).
