Orbital Decay Estimator

A rough estimate of how long the thin upper atmosphere takes to drag a satellite down until it re-enters, from its orbit and how heavy it is for its size. Treat the answer as a range, not a date: the density of the upper air, which sets the answer, rises and falls several times over with the Sun's activity.

How this is worked out

Air density. The density comes from the exponential atmosphere table in Vallado's Fundamentals of Astrodynamics and Applications (4th ed., chapter 8, Table 8-4), built from the COSPAR International Reference Atmosphere 1972 (CIRA-72) for average solar activity: in each altitude band, ρ = ρ0 · e−(h − h0)/H, with H the band's scale height (the climb over which the density falls by a factor of e, about 2.7).

Decay. Drag is a force against the direction of travel, ½ ρ v² / B per unit mass, where B = m/(CdA) is the ballistic coefficient: heavy, compact objects have a high B and come down slowly. Its effect on the orbit's size (semi-major axis) and shape (eccentricity) follows from Gauss's equations for a slowly changing orbit, averaged over each orbit and stepped forward in time. For a circular orbit they reduce to da/dt = −ρ F √(μa) / B. F is King-Hele's correction for the air turning with the Earth, (1 − r ω cos i / v)², about 0.9 for a prograde low orbit. An elliptical orbit loses height mostly at apogee, so it grows rounder as it decays. The estimate ends when the whole orbit is below 120 km, after which re-entry takes hours or less.

How far to trust it. This is a rough estimate. The table has no solar cycle in it: real density at 300 to 600 km swings by roughly five to ten times between solar minimum and solar maximum, and jumps further in geomagnetic storms, so a real lifetime can easily be half or double the central figure, and over a long lifetime spanning a solar cycle the error can be larger still. That is why the results also show the lifetime with the density doubled (towards solar maximum) and halved (towards solar minimum); those two are a plain factor-of-two bracket, not a solar model. Other things left out: the Earth's flattening, the day-night bulge in the atmosphere, attitude changes, and the pull of the Moon and Sun, which matters for orbits with a high apogee. For a real re-entry prediction, use the official tracking centres' messages.

References: Vallado, Fundamentals of Astrodynamics and Applications, 4th ed., chapter 8 (atmospheric drag, Table 8-4) and chapter 9 (Gauss's variation of parameters); King-Hele, Satellite Orbits in an Atmosphere: Theory and Applications (1987). Test: tests/orbital-decay.test.js (450 km circular with B = 100 kg/m² falls 71.44 m a day, reaches 400 km after 471.4 days and 350 km 194.3 days later, matching the closed form for each band).
