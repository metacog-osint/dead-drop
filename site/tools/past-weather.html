Past Weather

What the weather was at a place and time in the past, from two free archives. The first is ERA5 reanalysis: a weather model re-run over the past with the observations of the day, giving an hourly estimate for any point on Earth since 1940, but averaged over a grid square about 30 km across. The second is METAR reports, the routine weather observations airports send every hour or so, measured at the airport itself. Use the reanalysis where there is no airport nearby; use the airport reports when one is close, because they are real measurements.

Upper air

Soundings, the weather balloon profiles of temperature, humidity and wind with height, are launched twice a day (00 and 12 UTC) from a few hundred places. This page does not fetch them. Two free archives to look them up by station and time: University of Wyoming, upper air and Iowa Environmental Mesonet, RAOB archive.

How this is worked out

Reanalysis and observation

A reanalysis runs one fixed version of a weather forecast model over past decades, pulling it towards the observations that were made at the time (stations, balloons, ships, aircraft, satellites). The result is a complete hourly record everywhere, including where nobody measured anything, but it is still a model: each value stands for a grid square about 30 km across and the model's own terrain, not for a spot. An observation is a measurement at one place, here an airport's weather station, and is the better record for that place. Where both exist, the airport report wins for that airport; the reanalysis fills the gaps between airports.

ERA5 through Open-Meteo

The page asks Open-Meteo's Historical Weather API (archive-api.open-meteo.com) for the ERA5 model (models=era5) at your latitude and longitude, for the UTC days covering your time plus and minus the hours you chose, and takes the hour nearest your time (on a tie, the earlier hour). Open-Meteo answers for the grid point it used; the page shows that point and how far it is from yours. ERA5 is published about five days behind real time, so the last few days may be empty. Temperature and dew point are 2 m above the ground, wind 10 m above it; precipitation and snowfall are totals for the hour before the time shown; cloud cover is the percentage of the sky. The "weather" words are Open-Meteo's code (WMO table 4677) worked out from the model's cloud and precipitation, not something anyone saw. Wind is asked for in knots.

METAR through the Iowa Environmental Mesonet

The page asks the Mesonet's ASOS download service (mesonet.agron.iastate.edu, cgi-bin/request/asos.py) for the station's routine and special reports over the window, as comma-separated text in UTC, and decodes the one nearest your time. The decoding follows WMO No. 306, Manual on Codes, FM 15 METAR and FM 16 SPECI, and the US forms in the FAA's Aeronautical Information Manual, section 7-1:

  • Wind: direction the wind blows from, in degrees true, speed and gust in knots (m/s and km/h converted: 1 kt = 1.852 km/h); VRB is variable, and dddVddd the range it varies over.
  • Visibility: metres (9999 is 10 km or more) or US statute miles (1 SM = 1,609.344 m; P6SM is more than 6, M1/4SM less than a quarter). CAVOK means visibility 10 km or more, no cloud below 5,000 ft and no significant weather. Runway visual range (R groups) is listed as sent.
  • Weather: intensity (- light, + heavy, VC in the vicinity), descriptor and phenomenon from WMO code table 4678, such as -SN light snow, +TSRA thunderstorm with heavy rain (the intensity is the rain's), FZFG freezing fog.
  • Cloud: FEW, SCT, BKN, OVC with the base in hundreds of feet above the airport, CB (cumulonimbus) or TCU (towering cumulus); VV is the vertical visibility into fog or snow. The ceiling is the lowest broken or overcast layer, or the vertical visibility (14 CFR 1.1).
  • Temperature / dew point in whole °C, M for minus. In US remarks the T group gives both to a tenth of a degree (T11221150 is -12.2 / -15.0 °C), and SLP the sea-level pressure (SLP215 is 1,021.5 hPa, SLP982 is 998.2 hPa), per the Federal Meteorological Handbook No. 1, chapter 12.
  • Altimeter: A in hundredths of an inch of mercury (A3002 is 30.02 inHg) or Q, the QNH, in hectopascals; converted with 1 inHg = 33.8639 hPa. The altimeter setting is pressure reduced to sea level by the standard atmosphere and is not the same figure as the sea-level pressure in remarks.

Groups the page does not decode (recent weather, wind shear, runway state, most remarks) are listed as they came, never guessed. Trend groups (NOSIG, BECMG, TEMPO) are shown as text.

Derived values

RH = 100 · es(Td) / es(T),  es(T) = 6.1094 · exp(17.625 T / (T + 243.04)) hPa

Relative humidity, how close the air is to saturation, from the temperature and dew point by the Magnus formula with the constants of Alduchov and Eskridge (1996), within about 0.4 % of the exact saturation vapour pressure between -40 and 50 °C over water. Open-Meteo's own humidity figure is shown beside it.

Wind chill (°F) = 35.74 + 0.6215 T − 35.75 V^0.16 + 0.4275 T V^0.16

The US National Weather Service wind chill (2001), T in °F and V the 10 m wind in mph, shown only at 50 °F (10 °C) or below with wind above 3 mph, where it is defined. The heat index, how hot it feels in the shade with a light wind, is the NWS equation (Rothfusz regression, with Steadman's simple form below 80 °F and the NWS's adjustments for very dry and very humid air), shown from 80 °F (26.7 °C), where the NWS chart begins. Both are worked from the 2 m temperature and 10 m wind, as the NWS does. A heat index of 103 °F (39.4 °C) or more is shaded: the NWS calls that range "danger".

Services and their terms

Open-Meteo: free without a key for non-commercial use, within its daily request limits; its data are licensed CC BY 4.0 and must be credited, as on this page. ERA5: Hersbach et al. (2020), "The ERA5 global reanalysis", Quarterly Journal of the Royal Meteorological Society 146, 1999-2049; Copernicus Climate Change Service.
