Launch Azimuth Calculator

The compass heading a rocket flies at liftoff (its launch azimuth, measured clockwise from true north) to put a payload into an orbit of a chosen inclination, the tilt of the orbit against the equator. Every reachable inclination has two headings, one going north-east (or north-west) and one going south-east (or south-west). The tool gives both, first for a non-rotating Earth and then corrected for the Earth's spin, and draws them from the launch site.

Positions are rounded to about a kilometre; pick one or type your own.

How this is worked out

Where an orbit crosses the launch site's latitude, the equator, the orbit and the site's meridian form a right spherical triangle. Napier's rules give

cos i = cos(latitude) × sin(azimuth)    so    sin(azimuth) = cos i / cos(latitude)

The north-going launch flies that azimuth; the south-going launch flies 180° minus it. Both reach the same inclination. This is the inertial azimuth, the heading in a frame that does not turn with the Earth.

On the pad the rocket already moves east with the ground, at ω N cos(latitude): ω is the Earth's rotation rate, 7.292115 × 10⁻⁵ rad/s, and N cos(latitude) the site's distance from the Earth's axis on the WGS 84 ellipsoid (465 m/s at the equator, 409 m/s at Cape Canaveral). The vehicle has to supply only the difference between the orbital velocity and that ground velocity, so the heading it flies relative to the ground is

vN = v cos Az,   vE = v sin Az − ω N cos(latitude),   azimuth relative to the ground = atan2(vE, vN)

where v = √(μ / r) is the circular orbital speed at the chosen altitude (μ = 398,600.4418 km³/s²). The rotation gain is the part of the ground's speed that lies along the orbit, ω N cos(latitude) × sin Az; it is negative for a retrograde orbit, which has to cancel the Earth's spin.

Limits. A direct launch can only reach inclinations from the site's latitude up to 180° minus the latitude; anything else needs a plane change after launch (a dogleg turn during ascent or a burn in orbit), which costs fuel and is not modelled here. The correction uses the site's speed at liftoff and the orbital speed at insertion, and ignores the minutes of flight between them, gravity, drag and steering, so real flight azimuths differ by a degree or so. Many headings are also ruled out by range safety (no overflight of populated land), which this tool does not know about. Site positions are rounded and come from public sources.
