Where to point an antenna at a satellite, and how far its radio frequency moves because of the Doppler effect: while the satellite comes towards you its signal arrives higher than it was sent, and while it moves away, lower. How this is worked out

The SGP4 model (satellite.js 5.0.0, MIT licence) gives the satellite's position and velocity in the non-rotating TEME frame. Both are turned into the Earth-fixed frame, where your antenna is at rest; the velocity loses the Earth's rotation, vfixed = R(θ) vTEME − ω × r. The range rate is how fast the distance from you to the satellite is changing, the satellite's velocity along the line of sight. The first-order Doppler formula then gives

fheard = fsent (1 − ṙ / c)    shift = −f ṙ / c    ftransmit, uplink = fchannel / (1 − ṙ / c)

with ṙ negative while the satellite approaches. At 145.8 MHz a satellite about 400 km up shifts by up to roughly ±3.3 kHz near the horizon; the shift changes fastest when the satellite is highest. The relativistic term, about (v/c)² = 6 × 10−10 of the frequency, is left out (under 0.1 Hz at 145 MHz). The satellite's own oscillator error is not known and is not included.

Pass times use the same search as the Satellite Pass Predictor (30-second steps, crossings refined to half a second); the Doppler table samples the current pass, or the next one, every 30 seconds.

The frequency presets are published channels for amateur, weather and navigation satellites (GLONASS L1 is given at its centre, channel 0; each satellite is offset by its channel number × 0.5625 MHz). Satellites fail and change modes, so check a current source before relying on one.

References: Montenbruck and Gill, Satellite Orbits, section 6.2.3 (range-rate measurements); Vallado, Fundamentals of Astrodynamics and Applications, 4th ed., sections 3.7 (Earth-fixed velocity) and 4.4 (range rate). Test: tests/rf-doppler.test.js (145.8 MHz approaching at 1 km/s: +486.336 Hz; a satellite moving square to its own radius still has a range rate of 5.0513 km/s seen from the ground).
